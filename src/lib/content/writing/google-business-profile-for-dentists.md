A patient finds your practice on Google. Before they open your website, they may check whether you're open, look at the entrance, read a few reviews, and tap the phone number.

Every one of those details can help them take the next step. Every one can also send them somewhere you didn't intend: the wrong door, an old phone number, a booking page that only works for existing patients.

That's the job of your Google Business Profile: help the right person find the practice, understand enough to choose it, and reach your team.

This guide covers the whole job for an independent dental practice, from finding the right listing and securing access to handling reviews, checking appointments, and keeping the information accurate. If you already have a profile, use it as an audit. You don't need to start over.

## Check the essentials first

If you're opening your profile alongside this guide, work through these questions first:

- Can the practice owner get into the account and manage access?
- Does the listing represent the practice, an individual dentist, or an accidental duplicate?
- Are the name, address, map pin, phone number, and hours right?
- Do the categories and services describe the care you actually provide?
- Can a new patient use the phone, website, and appointment links?
- Is someone responsible for reviews, changes, and checking the public result?

A completed field is only the beginning. The useful test is whether the information helps someone do what they came to do.

## What your profile does for local search

Google Business Profile, formerly Google My Business, is the listing you manage on Google Search and Maps. Google's [Business Profile FAQ](https://business.google.com/en-all/support/) confirms that it is free. A company can charge for managing it, but that is a fee for its work. Google's [rules for management companies](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7353941?hl=en) require that distinction to be clear.

Google describes three main influences on local results:

- **Relevance:** how well the business matches the search.
- **Distance:** how close it is to the person searching.
- **Prominence:** how well known it is, including information such as links and reviews.

Complete, accurate information helps Google understand the practice. More reviews and positive ratings can also help. Google does not publish a formula that turns a particular category, review count, or posting schedule into a guaranteed position. Its [local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) also says you cannot pay Google for a better local ranking.

My practical priority is to make the listing accurate and useful before spending time on small optimization experiments. Correcting a phone number has a clear purpose even when you cannot measure its effect on rankings.

If the profile is accurate but you aren't appearing for a particular search, use the separate guide to [why a dental practice isn't showing up on Google](/writing/why-isnt-my-dental-practice-showing-up-on-google). That is a different question from whether the listing is set up correctly.

## Find the existing profile and secure access

Search Google Maps for the practice's name, address, and dentists' names before creating anything. Write down the profiles you find and what each one represents.

If you already manage the profile, sign in with that Google account and find the practice in Search or Maps to open its [management controls](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3039617?hl=en).

Then choose the appropriate starting point:

1. **The practice has an unclaimed profile.** Claim and verify that listing.
2. **Someone else manages the practice profile.** Request access to it.
3. **You already have access.** Check who the primary owner is and who else can make changes.
4. **The practice genuinely has no listing.** Add a new profile.
5. **The profile is suspended or marked as a duplicate.** Resolve that problem through the relevant process.

Google's [duplicate-profile guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/12756178?hl=en) explains these distinctions. Creating another listing because you cannot find the password can leave you with a second problem.

### If an old vendor or employee controls it

Use Google's [ownership request process](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4566671?hl=en). The existing owner has three days to respond. If they do not, Google may offer an option to claim or verify the profile; that option is not guaranteed.

Keep the confirmation email and follow the status of that request. An access dispute is easier to manage when you have one clear record of what you requested.

### Set up access so it survives a staff change

For the practice's profile, I recommend keeping primary ownership with the practice owner through an account the practice can reliably recover. Give staff and outside help individual access.

Google distinguishes **owners**, who can manage users, from **managers**, who can handle most everyday updates. Its [access documentation](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3403100?hl=en) says each person should use their own Google account, so there is no need to share a password.

Record which account owns the profile, who handles routine changes, and who can help if that person is away. Remove former staff and vendors when their access is no longer appropriate. This is the same continuity problem covered in [changing dental website companies](/writing/changing-dental-website-companies): the practice needs usable access when a working relationship ends.

## Practice profile or individual dentist profile?

This is where dentistry needs more care than a generic small-business checklist.

Google allows profiles for public-facing individual practitioners who can be contacted directly at the verified location during their stated hours. Its [practitioner guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en#individual-practitioners) distinguish these situations:

<tr><td>One public-facing dentist at a branded practice</td><td>Google recommends one shared profile, named in the format “Practice brand: Dentist name.”</td></tr>
<tr><td>Several eligible dentists at one location</td><td>The practice can have its own profile, separate from individual practitioner profiles. Practitioner names should identify the dentist without adding the practice name.</td></tr>
<tr><td>One dentist offering several specialties or treatments</td><td>Do not create a different practitioner profile for every specialization.</td></tr>
</tbody>

An eligible dentist's profile and the practice's profile are not automatically duplicates just because they share an address. Conversely, a second listing for the same practice does not become legitimate because you give it a different treatment name.

Before requesting a merge or removal, identify the entity each listing represents. Use Google's [duplicate-resolution process](https://support.google.com/business/answer/12756178?hl=en) for actual duplicates.

### What to record when an associate joins or leaves

Keep a small inventory: profile name and link, practice or practitioner, current location, account access, and who is responsible for updates.

When an associate leaves, work with the dentist to establish what needs correcting on their profile and on the practice's. Do not rename a departing dentist's profile after the replacement or assume its reviews belong to the practice. If the history is unclear, resolve it with Google support before making irreversible changes.

The useful outcome is that a person searching for either the practice or the dentist reaches the right place. Creating more listings is not an objective in itself.

## Verify the practice without exposing patient information

Google chooses the [verification methods available to a profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7107242?hl=en). Depending on the business, these can include phone or text, email, a video recording, a live video call, or mail. More than one method may be required.

Follow the options shown in your account. A guide that tells every dentist to wait for a postcard, or every dentist to make a video, is too rigid.

If Google gives you a verification code, enter it yourself in Google's verification flow. Google says not to share that code, including with people who manage your profile.

### If Google requests a video recording

Google's [video requirements](https://support.google.com/business/answer/14271705?hl=en) call for one continuous, unedited recording of at least 30 seconds, recorded and submitted through the Business Profile on a mobile device.

For an office where patients visit, show:

- **The location:** a street sign, building number, or recognizable surroundings.
- **The practice:** permanent signage with the same business name shown on the profile.
- **Your authority to manage it:** access to a staff-only area or something only an authorized person could access.

Keep other people's faces and private information out of the video.

For a dental office, I would plan the route while the rooms are empty. Put away sign-in sheets, appointment lists, prescriptions, charts, and anything visible on a patient-management screen. Unlocking an appropriate staff area is a better demonstration than opening a patient's record.

If verification fails, read the reason Google gives and address the missing evidence. A polished office-tour video is not a substitute for the verification process.

## Get the name, location, phone, and hours right

These are the details a patient will act on. Check them against the website and the way the office actually operates.

### Business name

Use the name the practice uses in the real world. Google's [editing guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3039617?hl=en) ties it to signage, stationery, and branding.

For a fictional practice called Riverbend Dental, that means “Riverbend Dental.” Adding “Best Emergency Dentist, Implants & Invisalign Near Me” creates a different claim from the practice's actual name.

A service or place name can genuinely be part of a business's name. The test is whether it belongs to the established identity, rather than whether it might attract another search.

### Address and map pin

Include the correct suite or floor and inspect where the map pin lands. Google's [address guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/2853879?hl=en) covers both the written address and pin.

Then look at the arrival as a patient would. Is the entrance on another street? Is parking behind the building? Is there a different accessible entrance? Put those details on the website and show the entrance in your photos.

A conventional office where patients come to you is not a service-area business merely because patients drive from several towns. Google uses [service areas](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en) for businesses that visit or deliver to customers. Adding surrounding cities is not how you describe your patient catchment area.

### Phone

Use a number the practice controls and that reaches the intended team. Tap it from the public listing and check the number before placing a test call with the office's knowledge.

Check what happens after hours too. Does the message identify the practice and explain the next step? If urgent calls follow a different process, make the website's instructions agree with that process.

A correct number that leads to an obsolete greeting is still unfinished work.

### Hours

Set the ordinary weekly schedule and use special hours for holiday exceptions or short changes. Google provides [separate controls for those situations](https://support.google.com/business/answer/15300403?hl=en).

Don't present the office as open 24 hours simply because someone can leave a voicemail overnight. Explain the after-hours contact process separately.

Use split hours when the office closes for lunch. If you open on alternate Fridays, use special hours for the dates that differ from the regular schedule and explain the pattern on your website. Check the public display after saving, especially the next date when your schedule differs.

## Choose categories, services, and patient details carefully

### Categories describe the practice

Choose the most specific available primary category that accurately describes the business. Google's [category guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7249669?hl=en) recommends a few appropriate additional categories, rather than one for every service.

For a general dental practice, I would start by checking **Dentist** in the current category picker. A dedicated orthodontic or pediatric practice should choose the available category that reflects its actual specialty.

Offering a treatment does not, by itself, make the whole practice a specialist business. A general dentist who provides clear aligners should not choose a category that misrepresents their qualifications.

Review additional categories for the same reason. The question is whether they describe what the business is. Treatment detail has its own place.

### Services describe the care you provide

Use the services editor for actual treatments. Google's [services documentation](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9455399?hl=en) supports suggested and custom services, descriptions, and prices where available. Google says to keep prices and phone numbers out of custom service names; use the separate fields for prices and descriptions.

Write useful distinctions:

- “Dental crowns” can explain whether you offer same-day crowns, if you do.
- “Emergency dental appointments” can explain how someone should contact the office and that availability must be confirmed.
- “Children's dental care” can state which ages you see.

Those are examples of information a patient can use. Don't add treatments you refer out as though patients can receive them at your office.

If you publish a price, make the inclusions and limitations clear. Someone seeing a price for an exam should not have to guess whether imaging or a cleaning is included.

### Description

Explain the practice in plain language: who you see, what you provide, where you are, and what matters about the experience. Google's [description guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/13682007?hl=en) also warns that automatically suggested wording needs an accuracy check.

Here is a fictional example:

> Riverbend Dental is an independently owned general dental practice in Lincoln, Nebraska. We see adults and children for preventive and restorative care, including exams, cleanings, fillings, and crowns. New patients can request an appointment through our website or call the office. Our team can help confirm insurance participation before a visit.

Use only the details that are true for your practice. Google's [editing rules](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3039617?hl=en) limit the description to 750 characters and prohibit URLs; the field should explain the business rather than focus on promotions.

### Insurance, accessibility, and other attributes

Complete the attributes offered to your profile accurately, particularly information about access. Google says [available attributes vary](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9049526?hl=en) by location and category. Check the entrance, parking, restroom, and other features before selecting an accessibility claim.

Insurance needs a little care. Google has [offered insurance-network information for US healthcare providers](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/making-healthcare-options-more-accessible-search/), but that does not establish that every dental profile has the same controls today.

If Google displays insurance information for your practice, check it. If your profile offers an Insurance editor, keep it current. Either way, keep an accurate explanation on your own website and give patients a way to confirm their specific plan.

For the wording, separate being in-network with a plan from helping someone submit an out-of-network claim. Our guide to a [dental practice's new patient page](/writing/dental-practice-new-patient-page) covers those distinctions and the other answers patients need before calling.

## Use photos and updates to answer real questions

Think of photos as an introduction to a place someone has never visited.

I would start with the building from the approach, the entrance, reception, a treatment room, and the people a patient will meet. An entrance photo is particularly useful when the practice is inside a shared building.

Google's [photo guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6123536?hl=en) calls for clear images that represent reality. Use the actual office and team. A stock photograph cannot tell someone which door to use.

Take pictures when patients are absent, check the background for records and screens, and follow the practice's privacy process for any identifiable person. Refresh photos when the space or team changes.

For posts, share something useful: a new dentist joining, a new service, or instructions about access during building work. Google's [post guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7342169?hl=en) explains the available formats and content restrictions. If hours change, update the hours field as well; a post does not correct it.

My recommendation is to post when there is something worth telling patients. I would prioritize a working appointment link over meeting an arbitrary posting quota.

Google says its [Q&A features vary and may change](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3039617?hl=en). Keep durable answers about appointments, insurance, access, and the first visit on your website, then check whatever question or answer features appear on your public profile. Don't make an essential answer depend on a particular Google tab remaining available.

## Make the appointment link work for a new patient

The website link and the appointment link have related but different jobs.

For a single-location practice, the website link can lead to the main site. The appointment link should take someone to the actual scheduling or appointment-request path for that office.

Google's [business-link policy](https://support.google.com/business/answer/13769188?hl=en) requires a dedicated destination for the business and the ability to complete the stated action. Sending “Book” to a generic page where someone must start searching again is a poor handoff.

Open the public profile on a phone and follow the link:

1. Does it identify the right practice and location?
2. Can a person who has never visited continue?
3. Are the offered appointment types appropriate for new patients?
4. Is it clear whether they are choosing a confirmed time or sending a request?
5. Can they reach the office if the online route does not suit them?

Some third-party links can appear automatically. Check all the appointment options shown, including the preferred one. Google provides instructions to [manage local links and request removal of a provider](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6218037?hl=en).

### A useful distinction: the page opens versus the request arrives

Imagine a fictional practice whose appointment button opens a portal asking for an existing patient login. The link loads, but a new patient cannot use it.

The first fix is to direct the appointment button to an appropriate new-patient path. The recheck is to follow that path from Google and, with the front desk involved, confirm that a clearly labeled test request reaches the right staff member. Coordinate the test so it does not occupy a real appointment slot.

If you only opened the form, record “form opens.” If the office confirmed receipt, record that. If the process still requires a callback, the patient-facing wording should say so.

This is the approach behind [checking whether your dental website is doing its job](/writing/how-to-tell-if-your-dental-website-is-doing-its-job): follow the task far enough to know what actually happened.

## Ask for reviews without steering the answer

Give patients a simple, optional way to share an honest experience. Google lets you [create a review link or QR code](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en).

A neutral invitation could be:

> If you'd like to share your experience, you can leave a Google review here. It's entirely optional, and we welcome honest feedback.

Use a consistent process that does not depend on whether the patient seems pleased. If you use email or text, use the practice's approved communication process.

Google's [review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en) prohibits incentives, selectively soliciting positive reviews, discouraging negative ones, and pressure to review while on the premises. It also prohibits requests for specific review content and staff review quotas.

That rules out a survey that sends satisfied patients to Google while directing everyone else only to a private form. It also rules out a discount for a review or instructions to mention a treatment or employee.

Make it easy to respond, then let patients decide whether and what to write.

### Public replies need a dental privacy boundary

A reviewer mentioning their treatment does not give the practice permission to disclose their information in response. The [American Dental Association's guidance on online reviews](https://www.ada.org/resources/practice/legal-and-regulatory/managing-dental-practice-online-reviews) explains that even confirming someone is a patient can create a privacy problem.

This has led to enforcement. HHS [settled with New Vision Dental](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement/agreements/new-vision/index.html) over disclosures of patient information in online-review responses and other potential HIPAA violations.

Avoid discussing attendance, treatment, insurance, bills, or details from the record in public. Route the concern through the practice's approved private process.

Check any response against your practice's privacy policy. A general reply might be:

> We take concerns seriously. Please contact our office so we can discuss them privately.

The practice should decide who can respond and what requires escalation. At Root, patient review responses stay with the practice; I can help with guidance, but I don't answer on your behalf.

## Measure interactions and appointments separately

Open the profile's Performance section, but read the labels carefully. Google's [metric definitions](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9918094?hl=en) distinguish these actions:

<table>
<thead><tr><th scope="col">Metric</th><th scope="col">What it records</th><th scope="col">What it does not establish</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Calls</td><td>Clicks on the call button</td><td>Answered calls or booked patients</td></tr>
<tr><td>Website clicks</td><td>Clicks on the website link</td><td>Completed appointment requests</td></tr>
<tr><td>Directions</td><td>Requests for directions</td><td>Actual arrivals at the office</td></tr>
<tr><td>Bookings</td><td>Completed bookings reported through a supported provider</td><td>All appointments made through every channel</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

Available metrics vary, and Google's performance reporting can include activity from ads as well as organic results. It is not automatically a count of unpaid new-patient leads.

Google also says [booking performance is unavailable for custom links](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7475773?hl=en). If your appointment button points to your own scheduler, check completed bookings in that system.

For the practice, I would keep three questions separate:

- Are people finding and interacting with the profile?
- Can they successfully contact the office or request an appointment?
- How many new-patient appointments were booked and attended?

Use the practice's own records for the last question. Asking how someone found you can add context, though a patient who says “Google” may not distinguish Maps, an ad, and an ordinary search result.

Look for a problem you can investigate. If website clicks are steady but requests fall after a scheduler change, test that path. Avoid dividing unrelated totals into a precise-looking conversion rate.

## Keep it accurate as the practice changes

Give this work a named owner. “The marketing company probably does it” is difficult to check when nobody can explain what changed.

My suggested routine is a regular public-profile check, plus an update whenever a real change occurs:

<table>
<thead><tr><th scope="col">Practice change</th><th scope="col">What to check together</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Holiday, closure, or new schedule</td><td>Google hours, website hours, and the phone greeting</td></tr>
<tr><td>New phone or scheduling software</td><td>Call button, every appointment link, and receipt of a coordinated test</td></tr>
<tr><td>Dentist joins or leaves</td><td>Team page, photos, practitioner information, and account access</td></tr>
<tr><td>Services or insurance participation change</td><td>Profile information, website explanations, and front-desk wording</td></tr>

Google can also update business information using reports from other sources. Its [edit-status guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038311?hl=en) explains accepted, pending, and rejected changes. Check the published result rather than assuming that clicking Save completed the work.

### Moving, buying, or renaming a practice

For a normal move, update the existing profile. Google's [review-transfer guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3098204?hl=en) says not to create a new profile solely because the location or ownership changes. Reviews remain for a same-name business under new management, and Google describes review transfer for same-name relocations.

A substantial rebrand or genuinely different business needs separate consideration. Before closing or replacing anything, establish whether the existing profile represents the continuing practice or an individual dentist. Don't promise a buyer that every listing and review will transfer identically.

## If something goes wrong, identify the actual problem

**You can't manage the listing.** Check the signed-in account and ownership. This is an access problem.

**The listing is visible but absent from one search.** Check the search and location before deciding the profile is broken. This is a visibility question.

**Your change isn't showing.** Look at its status. A pending edit is different from a suspended profile. Google's [edit-status page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038311?hl=en) explains how to tell.

**There are two listings.** Determine whether they represent the same practice or distinct eligible entities before requesting a merge.

**Reviews are missing.** Google documents [several possible causes](https://support.google.com/business/answer/10313341?hl=en), including policy checks, delays, and recent merges. Identify what changed before assuming that a vendor deleted them.

### If the profile is suspended

Read the stated reason and correct the underlying problem. Prepare evidence establishing the practice's name and location, such as registration, a business license, or a utility bill.

Google's [current suspension guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4569145?hl=en) explains the appeals process and evidence submission. Gather the material before starting, attach it as directed before submitting, and follow the status in the tool.

Don't create another profile to bypass the suspension. Google explicitly warns against that.

Do not assume that changing only one field a day protects a profile from suspension. Make accurate, necessary corrections and retain evidence for important identity changes. If you need help, ask the person helping you to explain the policy issue and proposed correction.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Google Business Profile free for dentists?

Yes. Google does not charge for the profile. You can manage it yourself or pay someone for the work of maintaining it. That management fee is separate from Google Ads and does not buy a better position in the map results. See Google's [third-party policies](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7353941?hl=en).

### Does a Google Business Profile replace a dental website?

They do different jobs. The profile gives patients a quick way to find the practice, check basic details, and take action. Your website gives you room to explain insurance, the first visit, your team, and how appointments work.

### How many Google reviews does a dental practice need?

There is no review count that guarantees a position in local results. Build a consistent, neutral way to invite genuine feedback and address the problems patients describe. Treat a competitor's review total as context, not a target that unlocks rankings. Google's [ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) describes several factors.

### Will we lose our Google reviews if the practice moves?

A move is not a reason to start a new profile. Update the existing practice profile. Google says reviews transfer when a business relocates under the same name, with exceptions for certain categories. A major rebrand or different business requires separate consideration. See Google's [review-transfer guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3098204?hl=en).

### How often should we update our Google Business Profile?

Update information when the practice changes, and give someone responsibility for checking the public profile regularly. A monthly review is a reasonable office routine, with extra checks before holidays and after changes to staff, hours, phones, or booking software. This is an upkeep suggestion, not a Google ranking requirement.

## What I would check before calling it finished

Open the public profile on a phone, outside the editing view.

Confirm that a person can recognize the practice, find the correct entrance and hours, reach the right phone number, and take an appropriate next step toward an appointment. Follow the website link far enough to find insurance and first-visit information.

For anything that fails, record what happened, who will fix it, and what would demonstrate that the fix worked. Recheck the same task after the change.

That's the standard I use for this work. The profile should agree with the website, and both should agree with the experience at the office.
