Your Google Business Profile matters more than your website for getting found — it's the largest single group of local ranking signals, and it's free. Setup is mostly straightforward. What isn't, and what this guide is really for, is the part where multi-dentist practices create listings for each dentist and quietly hand an associate the ability to walk off with the practice's reviews.

I'll cover the whole setup, but if you only read one section, read the one on practitioner listings.

## TL;DR

- **Primary category: "Dentist."** It outweighs secondary categories by a long way.
- **Never share your verification code** — Google says it will never ask for it, and neither should your marketing company.
- **Your business name cannot contain services or locations.** "Smith Dental — Implants & Cosmetic Dentistry of Austin" is a violation, not a gray area.
- **Practitioner listings are powerful and risky.** Google considers the _dentist_, not the practice, the true owner.
- **Q&A isn't gone.** It became an AI input in December 2025. Most guides get this wrong.
- **Don't put promotional pricing in Posts.** It's restricted content.

## Verification

Google determines your verification method automatically, and you can't choose it. If video verification is offered, Google itself recommends taking it — it's usually faster than a postcard.

**If you get video verification**, the requirements are specific and people fail them repeatedly:

- Unedited, unbroken, in one take, **at least 30 seconds**
- **Recorded live** from a mobile device through the Business Profile. Pre-recorded uploads are rejected.
- Must show three things: your **location** (street signs, building numbers, neighboring businesses), **proof the business exists** (your business name on a permanent fixture — a sign, a wall, a window — matching your profile name exactly), and **proof you manage it** (access to staff-only areas: the back office, the sterilization room, the front desk system)
- Must **not** include other people's faces. For a dental office that means film before opening or after closing. Don't film patients, and don't film staff who haven't agreed to it.

**If you get a postcard**, most codes arrive within 14 days and expire after 30. Two things invalidate it: editing your name, address, or category while you're waiting, and requesting a second code (which cancels the first).

One rule worth putting on a wall: **Google will never ask for your verification code, and you should not share it with anyone — including whoever manages your profile.** Google says this explicitly. Marketing companies ask for it routinely. A verification code is how someone takes control of your listing.

## Categories

Google's guidance is short: use as few categories as possible, make them as specific as possible, and don't use categories as keywords. The test is whether the category completes "this business **is** a," not "this business **has** a."

Dentistry has more available categories than almost any vertical — somewhere around a dozen. That's a trap more than an opportunity.

**For a general practice, your primary category should be "Dentist."** That's where the search volume is, and the primary category outweighs your secondary categories substantially. Local search specialists who test this on dental clients report that "Dentist" is the right primary in the overwhelming majority of cases, including one documented instance of a practice switching from "Cosmetic Dentist" to "Dentist" and ranking better for _dentist_ without losing its cosmetic rankings.

Add secondary categories that genuinely describe what you are. Don't add nine because the slots exist. And note Google's own caution: adding or editing a category can trigger re-verification, so change them deliberately.

You'll read that the primary category carries "60–70% of the weight" and secondaries "30–40%." That's invented — no study produces those numbers. What's supportable is that primary category ranks first among all local factors in the 2026 expert survey and additional categories rank eighth.

## Your business name

This is where dentists get in trouble more than most industries. In one study of over 5,000 listings across 17 industries, Google had corrected keyword-stuffed names on **4.49% of dentist listings — fifth highest of all industries measured.**

Google's rule: your name should be your real-world business name, as used consistently on your storefront, website, and stationery. Not permitted, and grounds for suspension: marketing taglines, phone numbers or URLs, all-caps words, legal terms like LLC or INC, location information, and — the one that catches dental practices — **service or product information**.

Google's own example is "Midas Auto Service Experts," which should be "Midas."

So: "Riverbend Dental" is fine. "Riverbend Dental — Implants, Invisalign & Cosmetic Dentistry of Austin" is a violation. If the longer version is genuinely on your signage and your business cards, Google does allow you to submit real-world proof. If it isn't, take it off before someone reports you.

## Services, attributes, and one thing that doesn't exist

**Services are worth filling in properly.** A local search agency tested whether Services affect ranking in 2019 and found no effect, then retested in 2022 and found a significant one — and published the reversal, which is rarer than it should be. Their finding: it moves rankings for both explicit and implicit keywords, more dramatically for explicit ones, and takes roughly 24–72 hours to take effect.

List your actual services, and keep prices and phone numbers out of custom service names — Google auto-rejects those.

**Attributes** cover accessibility (wheelchair-accessible entrance, restroom, parking), payments, amenities, and business identity. The accessibility ones are worth completing properly; they're genuinely useful to patients and almost nobody fills them in.

**One correction:** you will read in dental marketing articles that you can list accepted insurance on your profile. I went looking for that field in Google's attribute documentation and **it isn't there.** Payment attributes exist; an insurance attribute does not appear to. Put your insurance list on your website instead, stated plainly — it's the biggest content gap I see, and financial questions are the second-largest reason patients don't book.

## Q&A didn't disappear

Most guides currently say the Q&A feature was discontinued. That's a misreading.

In December 2025 Google replaced the customer-facing "Ask a question" flow with an AI-powered "Ask" button in Maps, which answers from your profile, your existing answers, and your reviews. Separately, the Q&A _API_ was deprecated in November 2025 — and that's the date that got reported as "Q&A is dead."

A Google product specialist confirmed in the official forums that the back end remains: you can continue to answer existing questions in your profile, and published questions and answers continue to power Google's understanding of your business and may still be shown in Maps.

So seeding plain answers to the questions patients actually ask still matters. It just feeds an answer engine now instead of a visible list — which, if anything, makes clear factual phrasing more valuable. Same logic as [AI search generally](https://root.site/writing/will-chatgpt-and-gemini-send-you-patients).

Other stale guidance to ignore: the GBP website builder was discontinued in March 2024, and in-profile messaging and call logs both ended the same year.

## Practitioner listings: the part that goes wrong

This is the highest-stakes section on the page.

Google permits individual practitioners — and names dentists explicitly — to have their own Business Profiles. The rules, from Google's guidelines:

- A practitioner should have their own profile if they're **public-facing** and can be **contacted directly at the verified location** during stated hours. **Support staff should not create profiles.**
- A practitioner **shouldn't have multiple profiles** for different specializations.
- Where multiple practitioners work at one location, the organization creates a profile for the location, and **each practitioner profile's title should include only the practitioner's name — not the name of the organization.**
- If a practitioner is the _only_ public-facing practitioner and represents a branded organization, they should share a single profile named `[brand]: [practitioner name]`.

Practitioners who test this add four rules Google doesn't state:

**Don't create one if your practice is named after that dentist.** If the practice is "Cole Smith DDS" and Dr. Smith wants a personal listing, you'll get ongoing duplicate flags. Create the practice listing and practitioner listings for the _other_ dentists.

**Don't duplicate the practice's primary category.** If your practice is "Dentist" and the practitioner listing is also "Dentist," Google's filters will suppress one of them and you've gained nothing while adding risk. The whole value is category differentiation — a periodontist listing ranking for implant searches, an orthodontist listing ranking for braces searches, each pointing at the matching page on your site.

**Don't name it "Riverbend Dental – Dr. Smith."** Google's text is explicit that multi-practitioner listings shouldn't include the organization name. That format gets rejected for quality.

**Pick people unlikely to leave.** Which brings us to the trap.

### What happens when the dentist leaves

Google treats **the practitioner, not the practice, as the true owner** of a practitioner listing.

If a dentist leaves and controls their listing, the correct action under Google's rules is to update it with the new location's information. Meaning: they take it with them — the reviews, the ranking history, all of it — and point it at their new employer. That is Google working as designed, and it is not good for you.

If your practice is the verified **owner** of the listing, you can ask Google to remove it entirely when they leave. Support reps sometimes push back, but it's the documented policy.

Two consequences worth acting on:

1. **Be the owner, not a manager, on every practitioner profile at your address.**
2. **Audit for practitioner profiles you don't know exist.** Local search agencies doing audits routinely find listings the practice had no idea were there — often created years ago by a previous marketing company or by the associate themselves.

And if you're buying a practice: Google cannot reassign a practitioner listing from one dentist to another. You either get access to it or you ask for it to be removed. Its reviews cannot be transferred to a different person.

## Two traps nobody mentions

**Rebranding.** Google's documented rule is that only _minor_ name changes are eligible — proper nouns and services unchanged, category unchanged. Anything more is treated as a new business, meaning you mark the existing profile closed and create a new one. **You lose the reviews.** The same applies to relocating. Practices rebranding after a buyout generally have no idea this is the stated rule.

**Promotional pricing in Posts.** Google's restricted content policy prohibits deals, coupons, and pricing information in uploaded content, and specifically flags health and medical devices as regulated. Dental practices post "Invisalign $500 off this month" constantly. That's a policy risk, not a growth tactic.

## If you get suspended

First, some reassurance: dentistry is not a high-risk vertical. In that same 17-industry study, 12.8% of ranking dental listings had been removed for guideline violations — sixteenth of seventeen. Garage door repair was 87.6%.

The most common trigger practitioners report isn't spam at all — it's **making many edits to important fields at once**, because that's exactly what a listing hijacker does. The practical advice: make one change a day over several days rather than overhauling everything in an afternoon.

If it happens:

- **Don't create a new profile.** The suspended listing carries your ranking history and reviews; a new one has neither, and only Google can move reviews.
- **Use the appeals tool.** The old reinstatement form was retired in February 2024. Have your evidence ready — business registration, license, tax certificate, or a utility bill — because once you open the evidence form you have **60 minutes** to submit it.
- **Submit once and wait.** Google says appeals take up to five business days. Practitioners handling these routinely report one to six weeks, so plan accordingly.

Incidentally, "soft" and "hard" suspension aren't Google terms — a consultant coined them, and support agents may not know what you mean. Google says suspended, disabled, restricted, or removed.

## One honest note on the completeness statistic

Every guide like this quotes Google saying complete profiles are 2.7x more likely to be considered reputable and 70% more likely to attract location visits.

I tried to trace it. It comes from a Google workshop deck, footnoted to "Ipsos research: Benefits of a complete listing 2017." That footnote links to a Google Sheet that returns "you need access." The study has never been published, and Google has put out at least two contradictory versions of the numbers.

Fill in your profile completely anyway — it obviously helps, and completeness ranks #16 in the expert survey. Just know that the industry's favorite statistic on the subject terminates in a spreadsheet nobody can open.
