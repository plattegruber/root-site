A patient has an appointment on Tuesday morning. They leave work early, put your practice into Google Maps, and drive to the building they've visited for years.

Your sign is gone.

The patient didn't miss your move announcement on purpose. They followed the directions your website or an old appointment reminder gave them.

That is the part of a dental office move worth planning around. Not how the announcement looks, but whether someone trying to see their dentist ends up at the right door.

A move affects more than the contact page. Your address can appear in your website header, Google Business Profile, appointment system, forms, automated texts, map links, and directories. Those systems don't all update together.

Here's how I'd handle the public-facing pieces of a move for an independent practice.

## 1. Write down the dates before editing anything

Start with one short document that the dentist and front desk agree is accurate.

It should answer:

- What is the **last date** patients will be seen at the old office?
- What is the **first date** patients will be seen at the new office?
- Is there a day or a longer period when neither location is seeing patients?
- What is the complete new street address, including building, suite, and floor?
- What is the practice's actual phone number during the transition?
- Where should patients park, enter, and check in?
- Who is responsible for changing each public system?

Keep the old and new addresses visibly labeled in this working document. Otherwise someone will copy the new address into a reminder for an appointment that is still happening at the old office.

If the move date is uncertain, don't announce a firm opening date as though it's settled. An honest "We're planning to move in December; we'll confirm the date before appointments are scheduled there" is better than a confident date you may have to walk back.

The simplest rule: **every patient-facing message needs to agree about where a particular appointment will happen.**

## 2. Announce the move before replacing the old address

You don't have to switch the entire website to the new address as soon as the lease is signed.

Before the move, a useful notice near your homepage's phone and appointment options might say:

> We're moving. Appointments through December 11 are at our current office on Oak Street. Beginning December 14, we'll see patients at 240 Pine Avenue, Suite 200. Your appointment confirmation will include the correct location. Call us if you're unsure where to go.

Those addresses and dates are fictional; use your real plan.

Add the same information to your contact page. Link to a dedicated moving-details section if parking or entrance instructions need more room.

Once the new location actually opens, change the primary address everywhere on the site and remove the expired announcement. You can leave a short "We've moved" note for a while if patients still need it.

Don't leave two unlabeled addresses in the footer. Someone glancing at the bottom of a page should not have to guess which office is open.

## 3. Update Google Business Profile without creating a duplicate

For a relocation of the same dental practice, Google's instructions are specific: **update the existing Business Profile's address rather than create a new profile for the new location**.

See Google's [guidance on relocated businesses and duplicates](https://support.google.com/business/answer/12756178?hl=en) and [editing a Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3039617?hl=en).

Changing the address of a verified profile can require another verification. Plan for that possibility rather than expecting the new address to appear everywhere instantly.

Ask whoever manages your profile to check:

- The exact address and suite number.
- The map pin, especially in a multi-building office park.
- The website and appointment links.
- Main hours and any closure or reopening exceptions.
- The photos and exterior information patients might use to recognize the building.

Google's [business representation rules](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) also require accurate real-world location information. Don't add extra keywords to the address or use a location that isn't the practice's actual office.

Once the edit is accepted, search for the practice like a patient would and use the **Directions** button. See where the route ends. A correct street address with a misplaced map pin is not really a finished update.

If you're opening an *additional* staffed office while keeping the original one, that is a different situation. This checklist is about moving one practice, not adding a second location.

## 4. Check every map link on the website

The address in your footer is only one place to look.

Your website may have a Directions button, an embedded map, a link in the new patient instructions, and a location mentioned inside a service page. Some map buttons contain the old address directly in the link. Changing the text beside the button won't change where it sends people.

On a phone, tap every route into the office:

1. The address or Directions link in the header or footer.
2. The map on the contact page.
3. Any parking or first-visit directions.
4. The appointment confirmation link, if the website sends one.
5. Any downloadable directions or new patient packet.

Make sure the destination is the new office and the entrance or suite instructions match what a person will actually see.

If the new building has an awkward entrance, a small photograph with a caption such as "Use the south doors beside the pharmacy" can be more helpful than a beautifully designed map. Only publish directions you've checked in person.

## 5. Your appointment reminders may matter more than the homepage

This is the system I'd put near the top of the list.

A returning patient may never visit your website before an appointment. They may rely entirely on the reminder sent by your practice management or scheduling software.

Review the actual messages, not just the settings screen. Ask the front desk to inspect a sample of reminders for appointments on both sides of the move date.

Check:

- Text and email appointment reminders.
- Confirmation emails and calendar invitations.
- Online scheduling confirmations.
- New patient forms and preparation instructions.
- Phone and voicemail messages.
- Any patient portal address or embedded map.

A reminder that says "See you tomorrow" can still contain a stale address or old map link underneath.

If you've already booked appointments for the new office, make sure those patients receive the correct location details. Coordinate any outreach with the practice's approved patient communication process; don't use an ordinary website contact form to collect medical information.

Before opening day, have a staff member test the path from an appointment reminder to the actual front door.

## 6. Don't move your domain just because you're moving offices

A physical move is not a reason to abandon your website address.

If your dental practice is keeping the same name and website, you can usually keep the domain and the page URLs exactly as they are. The changes are to the location information and the directions, not the entire website structure.

This distinction matters because a website migration and an office relocation are different jobs. If somebody proposes a new domain, redesign, or set of replacement pages solely because you're changing buildings, ask what problem that actually solves.

If some page URLs do change for other reasons, have your developer map the old pages to relevant new pages with permanent redirects. Google's [redirect guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/301-redirects) explains why. Don't send every old link to the homepage just because it's easier.

You want someone clicking an old appointment or service-page link to land somewhere useful.

## 7. Work through the listings you actually use

After the website and Google profile, make a list of the places patients and referring offices use to find you.

That may include insurance directories, your professional association directory, Apple Maps, Bing Places, your practice's social profiles, and the referral information shared with local clinicians.

Some of those accounts are managed by people other than your website provider. Assign an owner to each one and record when the public change is visible.

Start with the destinations your front desk actually hears about from patients. You don't need to buy a campaign to "fix every citation on the internet." You need accurate information in the places people use.

Also check the practice's letterhead, email signatures, printed appointment cards, and signage. They're not websites, but they can undo a lot of good digital work if they still point people to the old building.

## 8. Give patients the useful details about the new place

"We've moved to a beautiful new office" is a pleasant announcement. It doesn't tell a patient how to get inside.

A patient-first moving page answers the everyday questions:

- Is the phone number staying the same?
- Is parking free, and where is it?
- Which entrance is closest to the office?
- Is the office on the second floor? Is there an elevator?
- Do appointment times and doctors remain the same?
- Are there any temporary changes to scheduling or access?

Only answer what you know. If accessibility features or parking arrangements haven't been checked, verify them rather than guessing.

For an established practice, it can be useful to say what *hasn't* changed: same team, same phone number, same way to request an appointment—if all of that is true.

That reassurance will often mean more to a longtime patient than a gallery of the renovation.

## 9. Run the test from outside the building

Once the practice is seeing patients at the new location, have someone who didn't build the website try to find it.

Give them three tasks:

1. Search for the practice on Google and follow Directions.
2. Open the website on a phone and find parking and entrance instructions.
3. Follow the appointment link and check which location the confirmation shows.

Then ask them where they'd go if they had an appointment tomorrow.

If the answer is anything other than the new front door, you have a real problem to fix. It might be a stale reminder template, an old address inside a map URL, or one wrong sentence on the website.

Run that same check again after the first few days. Public profiles and third-party listings don't necessarily update at the same pace.

## One move, one clear answer

Moving a dental practice involves a hundred decisions. Your website doesn't need to explain all of them.

It needs to get one simple thing right: **when a patient is ready to come see you, can they find the correct place without calling to untangle the details?**

Start with the dates. Update your website and Google listing. Check the appointment messages. Then follow the directions yourself.

## Questions practices ask

### Should we make a new Google Business Profile at the new address?

Not when the same practice is relocating. [Google says to update the existing Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/12756178?hl=en), and an address change may require another verification. A second office that remains open is a different situation.

### When should the new address appear on our website?

Announce the upcoming move clearly before it happens, naming which dates belong to which office. Switch the site's primary address when patients actually begin visiting the new location. Check reminders for patients already booked on either side of the change.

### Do we need a new website because we're moving?

No. Moving offices doesn't normally require a new domain or website. Keep working pages and links in place, and update the address, contact information, map links, and directions.
