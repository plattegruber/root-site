Most likely because you're too far away from whoever is searching. Google names three local ranking factors — relevance, distance, and prominence — and distance is the one nobody selling SEO wants to discuss, because it can't be bought or optimized. A patient searching from across town sees a different map pack than a patient outside your door, and that's working as intended.

That's not the whole answer, and there are real fixable causes further down. But if you're searching from your own office and wondering why you rank first for yourself and nowhere for your patients, you've found it.

## TL;DR

- **Proximity is the #2 ranking factor** in the most recent expert survey, one point behind primary category. You can't change it.
- **Your primary category is #1 and entirely in your control.** For most practices it should be "Dentist."
- **Map pack position 3 is nearly worthless** — about 2.3% of clicks, versus 16% for position 1.
- **Check whether your site is indexed at all.** A leftover noindex tag takes you off Google completely.
- **Review thresholds jumped in 2026:** 68% of consumers now won't use a business rated under 4.0, up from 55% a year earlier.
- **Several statistics in every competing article are fabricated.** I'll show you which.

## The reason nobody wants to tell you

Google publishes its local ranking factors, and they're short enough to quote. Relevance, distance, and prominence. On distance, Google says: distance refers to how far each business is from the customer who's searching, and if a customer doesn't share where they are, Google uses what it knows about their location.

Then it adds, plainly: there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google.

Two of the top twelve factors are proximity. One of them is second overall, one point behind the primary category.

What this means practically: **your map pack ranking is not one number.** It's different for every patient depending on where they're standing. You rank first at your own address and progressively worse the further out you go. This is why the "we got you to #1" report from an SEO vendor is close to meaningless unless it says where the search was run from.

It also means some of what you're paying to fix isn't fixable. If a competitor sits in the dense part of town and you're out by the highway, you will lose searches from downtown no matter what either of you does. That's geography, not marketing.

The honest flip side: it also means you have an advantage nobody can take from you in your own neighborhood. The work is to make sure everything _else_ is right so you win the searches you're geographically eligible for.

## What actually decides it — and what you control

Group weightings from the same 2026 study: Google Business Profile signals **32%**, review signals **20%**, on-page **15%**, behavioural **9%**, links **8%**, citations **6%**, personalization **6%**, social **4%**.

So roughly half of local ranking is your Business Profile and your reviews. Both are free. Here's what I'd check, in order:

**Your primary category.** It's the single highest-scoring factor you fully control. For a general practice the right answer is almost always just "Dentist" — that's where the search volume is, and specialty categories tend to narrow you rather than add to you. Google's own test for choosing categories is whether it completes the sentence "this business _is_ a," not "this business _has_ a."

**Whether your address is in the city of search.** Factor #4. If your address is in a suburb with its own name, you may be structurally disadvantaged for searches that name the bigger city next door.

**Your hours.** Factor #5 is literally "business is open at time of search." If your hours are wrong on Google, you're invisible during the times you're actually open. This is free and takes two minutes and a startling number of practices have hours from three years ago.

**Your map pin.** Factor #10. Drag it to the actual door. On a multi-tenant medical building, Google's guess is frequently the parking lot or the wrong wing.

**Whether you have duplicate or suspended listings.** More on suspensions in [the Google Business Profile setup guide](https://root.site/writing/google-business-profile-for-dentists).

## Is your website even in the index?

This one is worth ruling out early, because it's binary and occasionally catastrophic.

A `noindex` tag tells Google to drop the page entirely from search results — Google's documentation says regardless of whether other sites link to it. Sites get built on a staging server with noindex applied across the board, and then somebody forgets to remove it at launch. I have found this on live dental sites more than once.

There's a nastier variant. If the page is _also_ blocked in robots.txt, Google never crawls it, never sees the noindex, and the page can still appear in results in a broken, contentless state. Google calls this out specifically, and notes that on platforms like WordPress and Wix the setting lives in a settings page rather than in the HTML, which is exactly why nobody thinks to look.

How to check: open Google Search Console, run the URL Inspection tool on your homepage, and look at the Page Indexing report. If you don't have Search Console set up, that's the first thing to fix — it's free and it's the only place Google tells you what it actually thinks of your site.

While you're there: a slow site won't get you removed, but it does hurt. I wrote about what actually makes dental sites slow in [why your dental website probably loads too slowly](https://root.site/writing/why-your-dental-website-loads-too-slowly).

## Reviews: the bar moved in 2026

If your ranking hasn't changed but your calls have dropped, this may be why. BrightLocal's 2026 consumer survey (n=1,002, published February 2026) found some sharp year-over-year moves:

- **68%** will only use a business rated 4.0 or above — up from 55% the previous year
- **31%** require 4.5 or above — up from 17%
- **74%** only care about reviews from the last three months
- **32%** look specifically at the last two weeks, up from 20%
- **47%** won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews
- **89%** expect owners to respond, and **50%** are put off by generic, templated replies

The recency shift is the one to act on. A practice with 200 reviews and nothing since spring now looks worse to a real human than a practice with 60 reviews and one most weeks. In the ranking factors, "recency of reviews" sits at #11 and "sustained influx of reviews over time, not bursts" at #14 — so Google and patients agree on this.

The fix is unglamorous: ask consistently. Five a month forever beats forty in a burst.

## About those statistics

Here's something that should make you skeptical of every article competing with this one.

You will read that the map pack gets 44% of clicks on a local results page. That number is fabricated. It traces back to a study where the raw click _counts_ out of 200 were misread as percentages and the labels got swapped — in the actual study, organic results won and the pack got about 21%.

You'll also see "the top 3 map pack results get 75% of clicks" (no study exists), "46% of all Google searches are local" (traces to a 2018 tweet about an unrecorded verbal remark by a Google employee), and "88% of local mobile searches result in a call or visit within 24 hours" (untraceable; the original citation link now resolves to a casino affiliate site).

What the evidence actually supports: the only primary click study of usable size — 5,500 testers shown real search results — found the local pack taking roughly **29–32%** of clicks and organic results **40–50%**. Within the pack, position one took about **16%**, position two **10%**, and **position three about 2.3%**.

Sit with that last figure. Third place in the map pack gets roughly one-seventh the clicks of first place. When a vendor reports that they've got you "into the top 3," that's a materially weaker result than it sounds.

That study is from 2018, on simulated desktop results, in one city, before AI Overviews existed. I'm citing it because it's the best thing available, not because it's current — and the fact that it's the best thing available after eight years tells you something about how much of this industry runs on vibes.

What _is_ current: about 68% of US Google searches now end without a click to any website. Reported Google Business Profile data for 2026 shows website clicks and calls down 12–16% year over year while direction requests are up 21–31%. And testing across 179 profiles found the new AI-powered local packs frequently showing **two** businesses instead of three, surfacing about a third as many distinct businesses as the traditional pack.

The map pack is getting smaller and more winner-take-all. That makes the free fundamentals more important, not less.

## The order I'd actually work through

1. **Confirm you're indexed.** Search Console, Page Indexing report. Binary, five minutes.
2. **Fix your primary category.** Usually "Dentist." Highest-scoring thing you control.
3. **Fix your hours, and your map pin.** Free, ten minutes, factors #5 and #10.
4. **Check for duplicate or suspended listings.** Including practitioner profiles you may not know exist.
5. **Make your name, address, and phone identical everywhere.** Google licenses third-party data, so bad data elsewhere feeds back into your profile.
6. **Start asking for reviews weekly.** Recency now beats volume for both Google and patients.
7. **Then, and only then, consider paying someone.** Most of the above is free.

One practical note on timing: Google says profile edits usually take up to 10 minutes to review but can take up to 30 days, and name and category changes are the most likely to get held. Practitioners also report that making many edits at once can trigger manual review, because that's the pattern listing hijackers show. So spread your changes across several days rather than fixing everything in one sitting.
