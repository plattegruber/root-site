Less than the people selling custom sites want you to think, and not in the places they claim. Template sites aren't inherently slower — in 2026 field data, the big template platforms actually outperform WordPress, which is what most "custom" dental sites are built on. And there is no credible evidence that custom sites convert better. The real difference is narrower and more boring: whether you can take the site with you, and how fast somebody will change something when you ask.

I build custom dental sites by hand, so I'm arguing partly against my own book here. That's fine. The honest version of the argument is stronger than the one the industry uses.

## TL;DR

- **"Custom" usually means semi-custom.** A vendor's own site explains it: build a base, swap elements, drop in your photos, and it gets "a more custom feel."
- **Templates aren't slower.** April 2026 Core Web Vitals data: Duda ~85% of sites passing, Wix ~80%, WordPress ~49% and last. Most "custom" dental sites are WordPress.
- **No study shows custom converts better.** The percentages you'll see quoted are fabricated. I looked.
- **Duplicate content isn't a penalty.** It just leaves Google nothing to rank you for.
- **The real differences are ownership and turnaround.** One vendor states in its own FAQ that you can't take the site with you when you cancel.

## What does "custom" actually mean here?

Start with definitions, because the word is doing a lot of unearned work.

Most dental vendors sell three tiers: pre-designed, semi-custom, and custom. The middle one is where most practices land, and Elevate DDS — a dental web company — describes its own semi-custom product with unusual candour: these are built from scratch by the marketing company, then the elements get rearranged and client photos and videos dropped into the existing page templating for each client, which "gives the websites a more 'custom' feel."

A more custom _feel_. That's an honest sentence and I'd rather practices read it than my characterization of it. Semi-custom sites run $3,500–$5,500 at that vendor. What you're buying is a shared skeleton with your photos in it.

You can also work out roughly how shared. DocSites advertises 1,000+ clients and 36 Standard designs. Distributed evenly, that's about **28 practices per design**. ProSites says it serves over 7,500 practices; it doesn't publish a template count. Great Dental Websites has built for 900+ practices since 2007 and openly sells Custom, Semi-Custom, and Pre-Designed as three separate products.

None of that is scandalous. It's how volume businesses work. But if a vendor tells you your site is custom and the invoice says $3,900, ask how many designs are in the catalogue.

Duda and Wix are template platforms. They're at the top. WordPress is at the bottom — and WordPress is the platform underneath the large majority of dental sites sold as custom.

The reason isn't that templates are magic. It's that hosted template platforms control the whole stack: they decide the hosting, the image handling, the script loading, and there's no plugin layer for anyone to bolt eleven things onto. A hand-built site can beat all of them, and mine do. But "custom" by itself buys you the ceiling, not the result.

The speed question still matters — Google's own research found that as a page goes from one second to three, the probability of a visitor bouncing rises 32%, and at ten seconds it's up 123%. It matters a great deal. It just isn't decided by whether your site started as a template. ## What about duplicate content?

You'll hear that template sites get penalized by Google for sharing text with hundreds of other practices. That's not how it works.

Google does not issue a duplicate-content penalty for this. What it does is pick one version as canonical and split the ranking signals across the copies. The practical damage is subtler than a penalty and, in a way, worse: if your "Dental Implants" page is the same 600 words that four hundred other practices are running, there is nothing on it for Google to rank _you_ for. You're not being punished. You're just interchangeable.

How much duplication is typical? Honestly, nobody has measured it properly. One agency reports seeing 30–40% duplicate content on template sites arriving from competitors, and another asserts 99% — but those are agency observations with no published methodology, from firms selling the alternative. I'd treat both as impressions rather than data.

What I'd rather point at is ProSites' own blog, which sells templates and still says: template pages can be copied an unlimited number of times, and that can be an issue when you're competing with other businesses. When the template vendor concedes the point, you can stop arguing about it.

The fix, incidentally, isn't necessarily a custom build. It's writing your own service pages about your own town, your own insurance list, your own hours. You can do that on a template.

## Does custom actually convert better?

I spent real time looking for evidence on this and came up empty, so I'm going to say so plainly.

You will see figures quoted: custom sites convert 47% better, or 12–18% better, or deliver 150% ROI over three years. Every one of those appears only on agency blogs and AI-generated content farms, and none of them cite a source. I could not find a credible study measuring conversion differences between custom and template websites in dentistry or in any other industry.

While we're here: the "94% of first impressions are design-related" statistic that appears in roughly every dental web design pitch traces back to a 2004 academic paper on trust in online health sites. The study had **fifteen participants**, all women, over four weeks. The number those researchers actually reported was 83%, and it referred to the share of _mistrust_ comments that mentioned design — not first impressions, and not 94%. The figure everyone quotes doesn't appear in the paper.

I'm not saying design doesn't matter. I'm saying the industry has been propping up a real argument with a fake number for twenty years, and you should discount any pitch that leans on it.

## So what is the real difference?

Two things, and neither is aesthetic.

**Can you leave with it?** This is the one that actually costs practices money. DocSites' FAQ says it directly: because their templates are proprietary, you may not take the website elsewhere after cancelling, though content you provided is yours. I'd rather a vendor say that plainly than bury it, and they did. But it means the site is rented. Five years in, if you want to move, you start over — new build, new URLs, and whatever ranking you'd accumulated goes with it. That's the expensive version of "cheap." The full version of that argument is in [do you actually own your dental website?](https://root.site/writing/do-you-actually-own-your-dental-website)

**How fast does it change?** A practice's website is wrong the moment a hygienist leaves, your hours shift, or you drop an insurance plan. On a template platform with a ticket queue, that's a form and a wait. What I've never found is a dental vendor willing to publish a turnaround time — I checked the major ones and not one commits to a number, though several promise "unlimited support."

Those two things are what I actually sell. Not that the design is bespoke, though it is. That you own the thing and it changes the day you ask.

## Which should you actually pick?

Genuinely depends, and here's my honest read:

**A template is the right answer if** you need something live in a hurry, your budget is tight, you're a new practice still working out your positioning, or your current site is so bad that anything decent is an upgrade. A good template site that loads fast and lists your insurance will beat a slow custom site every time. Get the fundamentals right first — that's [how dental practices get found online](https://root.site/writing/how-dental-practices-get-found-online), and none of it requires a bespoke design.

**Custom is worth it if** you're in a competitive market where every practice is running the same three templates, you have something specific about your practice that a shared skeleton can't express, or — most often, in my experience — you're tired of waiting a week for someone to change your hours.

**What I'd avoid either way:** a long contract, a proprietary platform you can't export from, and any vendor who won't tell you what happens when you leave.
