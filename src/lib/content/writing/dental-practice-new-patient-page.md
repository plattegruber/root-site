Someone finds your practice on Google. They like the photos. They see that you’re close to home. They tap “New Patients,” and the page says something about compassionate care and treating everyone like family.

That’s nice. But it doesn’t answer what they came to find out.

Are you taking new patients? Will their insurance work? What happens at the first appointment? Can they fill out the paperwork before they arrive?

Your front desk can answer all of that. The question is why it has to answer it over and over when your website could do most of the work first.

A good new patient page isn’t a second homepage. It’s a set of plain answers that makes the first call, and the first visit, easier.

## 1. Say whether you’re accepting new patients

Put the answer near the top, where someone can see it without scrolling.

“Welcoming new patients” is useful if it’s true. If you’re accepting adults but not children, say that. If you’re booked several weeks out, you can set that expectation without promising an exact date. If you’ve temporarily stopped taking patients, update the page.

Don’t make somebody call just to learn that you can’t see them.

This is also why the page needs to be easy to change. A sentence that was accurate at launch may be wrong six months later.

## 2. Make it clear how an appointment actually gets made

If patients call to schedule, show a phone number that they can tap on a phone. If they can book an appointment directly online, link to the booking system. If they can only _request_ an appointment, say “Request an appointment,” not “Book now.”

That distinction matters. Sending a request is not the same as having a confirmed time on the calendar.

If your team calls people back to finish scheduling, tell them that. You don’t need to promise a response in ten minutes. Just explain the process you actually use.

And don't bury the number behind a contact form. Sometimes a person would simply rather talk to your front desk.

## 3. Describe the first visit you really provide

Not the idealized version. The actual one.

How long should a patient plan to be there? Will you review their health history? Is the first visit generally an exam, or an exam and cleaning? Will X-rays be taken when clinically appropriate? Is there a conversation about treatment options before anything else is scheduled?

If the answer depends on the patient's needs, say so. For example, don't promise every new patient a cleaning at the first appointment if that isn't how your office works.

A couple of specific sentences are more helpful than a page of “personalized, comprehensive dentistry.” They also give nervous patients a clearer picture of what walking in will feel like.

## 4. Be precise about insurance and payment

“Most major insurance accepted” is one of those phrases that looks helpful but creates another phone call.

If you’re in-network with specific plans, keep the list current and name them accurately. Don’t assume that working with one insurer means you participate in every plan it sells. Let people know that your team can help check their particular coverage, and avoid guaranteeing what an insurer will pay.

For patients without insurance, say how to ask about self-pay pricing. If you offer a membership plan or payment options, link to the real terms. If you publish a new-patient price, be clear about what’s included and what could be separate.

You don't have to put every fee on the internet. You do have to give people an honest next step.

## 5. Tell patients what to do before they arrive

This should be the shortest section on the page.

What should they bring? A photo ID and insurance information, if your office requests them. Are new-patient forms sent after scheduling? Can they complete them through a secure patient portal? Should they arrive a little early if they plan to fill out paperwork in the office?

Keep the instructions tied to your real process. Don't ask someone to print six PDFs if your staff now sends digital forms.

One important boundary: a general website contact form is not the place to collect a patient's medical history, medications, or detailed dental symptoms. If your office gathers health information online, use its approved, appropriately secured intake process. The [American Dental Association’s patient registration guidance](https://www.ada.org/resources/practice/practice-management/patient-registration-and-forms) covers the information practices commonly collect and the privacy obligations involved.

## 6. Make finding the office boringly easy

Your address should be written out, not hidden inside a map. Include the suite number, the entrance patients should use, and any parking instructions that aren't obvious from the street.

If the office has an accessible entrance, explain where it is. If someone should enter through a different door after a certain hour, say that too.

Then check the hours against your Google Business Profile. A website that says you open at 7:00 while Google says 8:00 creates uncertainty before the patient gets to the chair.

None of this is exciting web design. It's the information a first-time visitor needs.

## 7. Explain what happens with urgent dental problems — if you see them

A patient with a broken tooth or swelling may land on the same page as someone looking for a routine exam. If your practice sees urgent cases, explain how to contact the office during business hours and what instructions you provide after hours.

If you don't offer emergency visits, don't imply that you do. Send patients to the appropriate contact or guidance your practice has approved instead.

A big “Emergency Dentist” button is a bad idea if nobody answers it on Friday night.

## What the page should look like

You don't need a complicated layout. Here's the order I'd use:

1. **New patients:** Are you welcoming them, and for which services?
2. **Call or request an appointment:** One obvious next step, with the right expectation about confirmation.
3. **Your first visit:** What usually happens and how long to allow.
4. **Insurance and payment:** Accurate plan information and a way to ask about costs.
5. **Before you arrive:** Forms, what to bring, and any preparation.
6. **Find us:** Address, parking, entrance, and hours.

That's enough for most independent practices. Add information only when patients genuinely need it.

The things I'd leave off: generic stock photography, a long mission statement, outdated insurance logos, and promises about first-visit services your team can't consistently deliver. Those are all easier to publish than they are to keep true.

## Try the front-desk test

Ask whoever answers your phone a simple question: _What are the five things new patients ask us most often?_

Open the new patient page on a phone. Can you find honest answers to those five questions in under a minute?

If not, those are your next five website edits. You don't need a marketing campaign to make them. You don't necessarily need a new website, either.

Then make somebody responsible for keeping the answers current. Insurance participation changes. Hours change. Forms change. A new dentist joins. The best page in the world is no help when the details are from last year.

That ongoing part is easy to underestimate. The page only earns its keep as long as it's right.

## Questions

**Does every dental practice need a separate new patient page?**

Not necessarily. A small site can put the same answers on its homepage and contact page. What matters is that a new patient can find them easily. A dedicated page becomes useful when those details would otherwise clutter the homepage.

**Should I list the dental insurance companies we work with?**

Yes, if the list is accurate and someone keeps it updated. Name actual network participation rather than making a broad promise about “all plans.” Patients should still confirm their specific benefits with the office and insurer.

**Should new-patient forms live on the website?**

The website can explain how to complete them and link to your practice's approved secure intake tool. The clinical history itself shouldn't be collected through an ordinary marketing contact form.
