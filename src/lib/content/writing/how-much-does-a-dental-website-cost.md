There is no single number, and anyone who gives you one without asking about your practice is selling something. But here are the real ranges: the ADA puts a basic customized dental site at about $6,000, and up to $25,000 for a high-end build. Meanwhile, actual published vendor prices start far lower — $1,999 plus $89 a month at one dental vendor, $97 a month flat at another. The spread is roughly sevenfold for something everyone calls "a professional dental website."

I build dental websites for a living and charge $149 a month with no setup fee, so I have an obvious stake in this question. I'll show you my sources so you can check my arithmetic.

## TL;DR

- **The ADA's own figure:** ~$6,000 for a basic customized site, up to $25,000 high-end, $10,000–$12,000 if a marketing firm builds it.
- **Real published vendor prices run much lower.** DocSites: $1,999 setup + $89/mo. Smile Sites: $97/mo, no setup. Both are dental-specific.
- **The three biggest names won't tell you.** ProSites, Officite, and TNT Dental publish no pricing at all as of 2026. I checked each directly.
- **The most-cited "independent" cost figure in dentistry was written by an agency owner** whose firm sells at the top of the range he quotes.
- **Hosting, SSL, and a domain cost $1–$20 a month wholesale.** Anything above that in a monthly fee is labor and margin — which is legitimate, but it isn't infrastructure.
- **Read the cancellation clause before the design portfolio.** That's where the real cost lives.

## What does a dental website actually cost?

Let's start with the most disinterested source available, because it's the only one in this market with nothing to sell you.

The American Dental Association's practice management guidance puts it this way: a basic but customized website runs around **$6,000**; a high-end site with additional features can reach **$25,000**; and if you hire a professional marketer to handle it, budget **$10,000–$12,000**. The ADA also suggests spending 5–10% of your total marketing budget on keeping the website updated.

## Why is it so hard to get a straight number?

Because most of the industry won't publish one.

In researching this, I went directly to the pricing pages of the three largest dental website companies in the country. ProSites' pricing page contains a quiz and a "book a consultation" button, and no prices. Officite, now under Henry Schein One, publishes nothing. TNT Dental gates its pricing guide behind an email form.

That's not a technical limitation. DocSites publishes a full price list including per-page add-on fees. Smile Sites puts a number on the homepage. It's a choice, and the effect of that choice is that you cannot compare options without sitting through three sales calls.

There's a second thing worth knowing. The single most-cited cost figure in dentistry — the "$3,500 to $30,000, budget around $14,000" range you'll find quoted everywhere — comes from a 2023 article in _Dental Economics_. That article was written by the founder of Rosemont Media, a dental web agency. The same numbers appear on Rosemont's own sales page, which is currently the top organic result for "how much does a dental website cost."

I don't think that's fraud. Trade publications run vendor bylines all the time, and the article discloses the author's firm. But it does mean the industry's benchmark price was set by someone selling at the top of it, and that essentially every article downstream has been quoting an agency's marketing copy as if it were market research.

The pattern is straightforward once you see it: **the infrastructure is nearly free, and the labor is nearly all of it.** A small static dental site costs somewhere between one and twenty dollars a month to host and secure. Every dollar above that is somebody's time or somebody's margin.

That's not a criticism. Time is the expensive part and it should be. But it does mean that when a vendor itemizes "hosting and security" at $89 a month, they are describing labor and calling it infrastructure. Ask what the hours actually buy.

## What about the monthly fee?

This is where the money quietly is, and where the fine print matters more than the number.

Three things to check on any monthly agreement:

**How many edits are included, and what happens after.** DocSites' $89 covers "up to 1 hour" of updates a month; extra content pages are $99 each and page migrations $50 each. Changing your template or color scheme after launch triggers a separate one-time fee. That's clearly disclosed, which I respect — but "hosting and updates included" and "one hour of updates included" are different products.

**Whether it auto-renews, and what leaving costs.** Dental Intelligence's published terms auto-renew for successive 12-month periods, require 30 days' notice before renewal to opt out, and specify that cancelling mid-term still leaves you owing the remaining contracted payments, none of it refundable. Progressive Dental requires a 12-month minimum on SEO with no refunds for committed spend. These are normal industry terms. They are also the reason practices stay with vendors they've stopped liking.

**Whether you can take the site with you.** DocSites' FAQ answers this plainly: because their templates are proprietary, you may not take the website elsewhere after cancelling — though any content you provided is yours to keep. I want to give them credit for saying so in writing, because most vendors don't. But read that sentence twice. On that model you are not buying a website. You are renting one, and the setup fee is a deposit on a rental.

## Is there a "dental tax"?

You'll hear that dentists get overcharged for websites relative to other small businesses. I went looking for evidence and couldn't find a study that measures it, so I'm not going to assert it.

What I can say is narrower and checkable. The ADA's $6,000 basic-site figure sits at the top of the general small-business band. And the recurring markup is real and computable: $89 a month for hosting, SSL, and an hour of edits, against $1–$20 of actual infrastructure cost, is a labor-and-margin charge dressed as a utility bill.

Whether that's a "dental tax" or just what service costs depends on whether the hour shows up. In my experience most practices are paying for an hour a month they never use, from a vendor who takes four days to answer email.

## What I'd ask before signing anything

Six questions, in the order I'd ask them:

1. **What is the total first-year cost, including setup, hosting, and any per-page or per-edit fees?**
2. **Who is listed as the owner of the domain, and in whose account does it live?**
3. **If I cancel, what do I take with me — the domain, the content, the design, the code?**
4. **Does the agreement auto-renew? What is the notice window, and what do I owe if I leave mid-term?**
5. **How many edits are included per month, and what's the typical turnaround?**
6. **Will you put the turnaround in writing?**

Question six is the one that separates vendors, and it's worth knowing that I could not find a single dental website company that publishes an edit turnaround commitment. Not one. Several promise "unlimited support"; none say how long support takes.
